What is a Story Palace?

A classic memory palace asks you to imagine walking through a building and “seeing” items placed in each room. Useless advice if you can't see anything. A Story Palace swaps the building for a story: a fixed sequence of numbered hooks — story beats built from a sound, a smell, a touch, an action, or a cause-and-effect turn. You attach each thing you want to remember to a hook. Later, replaying the story replays your list: the hook is the gateway that brings the memory back.

1
head ✋ touch/feel

You wake and lift your head — press a palm flat on top of it and feel the warmth there.

your item “Buy olive oil” → “I balance the slippery bottle of olive oil on my head.”

2
ear ♪ sound

A low hum reaches your ear; you tug the earlobe once to make sure you heard it.

your item “Call the dentist” → “The hum in my ear is the dentist's drill — better call before it gets closer.”

The science this is built on

  • Aphantasia is common and real. About 0.9% of people report no voluntary mental imagery, and another ~3% report very dim imagery (international prevalence study, n=9,063, 2024).
  • It's specific, not general. Episodic re-living is harder, but semantic memory, verbal memory, and spatial accuracy are intact — aphantasic students earn equal or better grades using compensatory strategies (Frontiers in Psychology, 2025).
  • Non-visual strategies are preferred and effective. People with aphantasia gravitate to spatial and sensorimotor strategies over rote verbal ones (“Unseen strategies”, New Ideas in Psychology, 2025), and memory-palace-style techniques work without visualization — memory researcher Lynne Kelly, herself aphantasic, is proof (Art of Memory).
  • Simple beats vivid. Aphantasic memory benefits from pictures as much as anyone's — and benefits most from simple symbols rather than rich photos (Journal of Vision, 2025). That's why this site runs on numbers, glyphs, and clean text.
  • Externalizing works. Writing the story down — not holding it in your head — is the point. Learn more about learning with aphantasia at aphantasia.com.

This site is an educational tool, not a medical instrument. The self-check is not a diagnosis.
